Knowing what the plant costs, while there is still time to change it

A maintenance and production intelligence system for Greenlam Laminates — what is built, what it is worth, and what to build next.

Master plan · v1 Phase 1–5 complete 25 August 2026

The argument

A register that only records is a cost. One that answers is an asset.

Greenlam's plants already keep a breakdown register. It is diligent, it is detailed, and by the time anyone reads it the press has been down for a week. The data exists; the answer does not, because nobody can pivot a four-thousand-row spreadsheet before the morning meeting ends.

This system does not replace that habit. It reads the same register, in the same shape, and turns it into the four sentences a plant head actually needs: which machines cost the most, what causes most of the downtime, whether it is getting better, and which machine is about to fail. Everything else in this document is downstream of that.

One discipline runs through the whole build, and through this plan: never print a number you cannot source. The dashboard refuses to show a rupee cost of downtime because nobody has given it an hourly rate per machine — it says so, in words, rather than showing a guess that would be quoted in a board meeting six months later. This document holds itself to the same rule. Public figures are cited. Plant figures are marked as missing. Nothing in between is invented.

Chief Financial Officer

The size of the prize

Greenlam Industries closed FY26 at roughly ₹3,000 crore of revenue, up 18% year on year, running about 24 million sheets of laminate capacity with a further 2 million being added, across five states. Management has reaffirmed a 16% laminate EBITDA margin guidance, with group operating margins guided at 11.0–11.5%.

Those numbers set the scale of what a percentage point is worth. At that capacity, every 0.1 percentage point of reject rate is roughly 26,000 sheets a year — scrapped after they have already absorbed decor paper, resin, press time and energy.

For comparison on the cost side: commercial maintenance platforms price at roughly $35–75 per user per month, with the AI capability reserved for the upper tiers. At even fifty floor users across one plant, that is a recurring line item before a single sheet is saved — and it buys a product that does not know what a laminate is.

Chief Marketing Officer & Chief Revenue Officer

Why buying this does not solve it

The maintenance software market is mature and the leaders are good at what they do. MaintainX won by making software technicians actually enjoy using, and now scans work-order history for anomaly patterns. Limble leads on sensor integrations and shipped AI resource planning and image-recognition asset capture. UpKeep is mobile-first and broad.

Every one of them is asset-centric. They model a machine, a work order, and a technician. That is the right model for a fleet of pumps in a water utility.

It is the wrong model for a laminate plant, and the reason is physical. In short-cycle pressing, blistering is in most cases the consequence of unstable impregnation and incomplete drying — it originates upstream of the press, not at it. Residual moisture flashes to vapour under the platen and lifts the layers. The defect is observed at Press-4. It was caused at IMP-7, hours earlier, on a different machine in a different section owned by a different supervisor.

An asset-centric system will log that as a Press-4 defect for ever. It will build a Pareto that says the press is the problem, a maintenance budget that re-plates the press, and a reject rate that does not move. The competitive opening is not a better work-order form. It is modelling the material as it flows between sections, so a reject can be attributed to where it was actually created.

The differentiated bets

Five things no competitor can easily copy

These are not features on a comparison grid. Each depends on knowing what a laminate is, which is precisely what a horizontal product cannot afford to learn.

  1. Cross-section causal attribution Manufacturing head Reject rate Carry the decor-paper lot and impregnation line forward with the sheet, so a blister found at the press is attributed to the impregnation run that caused it. Turns "Press-4 has a quality problem" into "IMP-7's drying was drifting on Tuesday nights". This is the flagship bet and the hardest to copy.
  2. Batch genealogy and complaint traceback CRO Warranty cost A distributor reports delamination on a batch. Walk backwards from the sheet to the press cycle, the shift, the resin batch and the paper lot in seconds instead of days — and know whether the rest of that lot is still in the warehouse or already in someone's kitchen.
  3. Hindi and Hinglish voice-to-ticket COO Adoption The single largest barrier to write-up quality is that a technician who diagnosed the fault perfectly does not want to type three paragraphs on a phone in a second language. Let them say it, in the language they think in, and extract the structure. The interface is already trilingual; this makes the input trilingual too.
  4. Retrieval over the plant's own history AI engineering First-time fix When a technician opens a ticket on Press-4, show the three most similar breakdowns from the register and what actually fixed them. Retrieval, not generation — it surfaces what a colleague already wrote and can be checked, rather than inventing a plausible repair procedure for a hydraulic press.
  5. Quality decay as a failure predictor Engineering head Unplanned downtime Reject rate rising on a machine before it fails is a free condition-monitoring signal — no sensor, no retrofit, no capex. The synthetic data shows the shape; whether it holds on real production is the most valuable single question the pilot can answer.

Chief Operating Officer

How this rolls out

Each phase ends at a decision gate with a stated pass condition. A gate that does not pass stops the rollout rather than escalating the budget.

  1. 01

    Import the history

    Upload the existing breakdown register. Nothing about the plant changes; the system simply learns what already happened.

    Gate. The Pareto built from your real register is recognisable to the plant head as a true picture of the last two years. If it is not, the import mapping is wrong and nothing downstream is trustworthy.

  2. 02

    One section, parallel running

    Press only. The register keeps being filled in on paper; the app runs alongside it for four weeks. Both are compared weekly.

    Gate. Reporting by scan is measurably faster than by paper, and at least three-quarters of breakdowns in the section get reported in the app without being chased.

  3. 03

    Whole plant, register retired

    All sections. The spreadsheet stops being the source of truth and becomes the nightly export instead — same headers, same pivot tables, no retraining for leadership.

    Gate. Root-cause quality above the threshold the plant sets, and the leadership workbook lands every night without anyone touching it.

  4. 04

    Prove the predictor

    Test the quality-decay-before-failure signal against real production. Publish the result either way.

    Gate. The correlation holds on real data, or it is retired publicly. A predictive claim that quietly stops being mentioned is worse than one that was never made.

  5. 05

    Second plant

    The multi-tenancy is already in the schema. This phase tests whether the process travels, which is the part that usually does not.

    Gate. The second plant reaches Phase 3 in half the elapsed time of the first.

Blocked on you

What only Greenlam can supply

None of these are engineering work. Each one is currently the reason a built feature is switched off.

  1. Hourly downtime cost per machine. Unlocks every rupee figure in the product. A costing exercise of about a day.
  2. Real shift timings. Currently a placeholder 3×8. Shift analysis is already the sharpest finding in the data and it is running on assumed boundaries.
  3. Criticality A/B/C per machine. A plant judgement, not a calculation. Drives response targets.
  4. Scheduled production hours. Turns availability from calendar-based — which the dashboard currently labels honestly as approximate — into a real OEE input.
  5. Written approval to host real plant data. Everything to date runs on synthetic data, enforced in code: the seeding script refuses to write to a non-local database. Nothing real moves until this exists in writing.
  6. One breakdown register file. The importer is built and tested. It needs a real file to prove the mapping against, and that is the Phase 1 gate.

Risks

What would make this fail

Write-ups decay

Root causes degrade to "belt issue" within a fortnight and the analytics describe nothing. Mitigated by the guided why-why and a quality score, but the real defence is voice input in Hindi.

Measurement becomes surveillance

The moment individual metrics are used to rank people, reporting stops. Enforced in code: individual figures are visible to that person and their direct supervisor only, never ranked, never exported.

The register keeps running

If the spreadsheet survives alongside the app, both are half-maintained. Phase 3 exists specifically to kill it, and the nightly export exists so that killing it costs leadership nothing.
